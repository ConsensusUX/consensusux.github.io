# Academic Foundation

ConsensusUX is grounded in a constellation of scholarly traditions. This page documents the key theoretical pillars and how they connect to platform design decisions.

---

## The Public Sphere (Habermas)

Jürgen Habermas's concept of the public sphere — developed in *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (1962/1992) — remains the foundational framework for understanding what ConsensusUX is trying to do and why existing digital infrastructure fails to do it.

Singh (2023) defines the public sphere as "a network for communicating information and points of view": a space situated between the private sphere of individual life and the domain of public authority (the state). The public sphere functions as a prerequisite for civil society — a discursive feedback loop through which citizens develop shared positions and hold power accountable.

Historically, the public sphere was instantiated in physical institutions: coffeehouses, salons, clubs, and voluntary associations where people could gather, deliberate, and form opinion outside of both family life and state apparatus. The key question for our era is whether digital space can perform this mediating function — whether it can sit between private life and public authority and enable genuine discursive accountability.

The contemporary answer is troubled. Knox, Nanopoulos, and Woodhouse (2025) offer a Marxist critique showing that existing legal frameworks — including EU law — embed racialization rather than neutrally enabling participation. More fundamentally, they argue that public authority is currently trending toward dissolution of accountability rather than its expansion. The digital public sphere, as currently constituted through commercial platforms, does not fulfill the Habermasian function. It aggregates attention without enabling deliberation, and it concentrates power rather than distributing it.

ConsensusUX is designed as an infrastructure experiment: can a digital platform actually perform public sphere functions by enabling discursive, consensus-based deliberation within and between groups?

## Civic Tech Movement (Schrock 2018)

Andrew Schrock's analysis of the civic tech movement (2018) provides both inspiration and a cautionary tale. Civic tech is defined by using technology "as a way to get more people involved in figuring out what the solution should be" — a fundamentally participatory impulse.

Yet the movement has significant limitations. Schrock identifies technocratic impulses within civic tech: a tendency to treat participation as a design problem solvable through behavioral "nudges" and interface optimization rather than as a political question requiring structural change. The scope of many civic tech projects is narrow — canonical examples like SeeClickFix allow citizens to report potholes but leave intact the political processes that determine road maintenance budgets and priorities.

Critically, civic tech created a disconnect between the civic and political spheres. It enabled participation in service delivery and community feedback while remaining separate from — and often unconnected to — actual political power. People could submit requests; they could not make decisions.

ConsensusUX takes the participatory impulse of civic tech seriously while rejecting its technocratic limits. The goal is not to make existing institutional processes more legible or frictionless, but to support groups in governing themselves through genuine consensus decision-making.

## Digital Parties (Gerbaudo 2021)

Paolo Gerbaudo's research on digital parties (2021) examines the most ambitious attempts to use digital platforms for genuine democratic participation within political organizations — most notably Podemos in Spain and Movimento 5 Stelle in Italy.

The findings are sobering. Despite rhetoric about horizontal participation, these platforms function primarily as plebiscitary tools: members are invited to vote on proposals that have already been developed by leadership, rather than participating in discursive formation of those proposals. Input is voting, not deliberation. The platforms serve as crowdsourcing mechanisms — legitimizing decisions rather than enabling collective authorship of them.

Transparency remains low despite the digital medium. Power remains centralized in platform controllers and party leadership. The gap between the participatory promise and the extractive reality is not incidental but structural: platforms designed for scale optimize for throughput (many votes quickly) at the expense of depth (genuine discursive engagement).

ConsensusUX responds directly to this failure. The platform is designed for discursive participation — proposals are developed collaboratively, consent checks are built into the process, and blocking is a legitimate and meaningful act rather than a deviation to be managed away.

## Consensus Decision-Making (CDM)

Consensus decision-making as practiced by Occupy movement assemblies and related horizontal organizing traditions provides the procedural core of ConsensusUX.

The Occupy tradition developed sophisticated mechanisms for large-group decision-making: spokes councils (where representatives from affinity groups deliberate), general assemblies (open to all), and modified consensus (where a supermajority threshold — typically 90% — is used in place of full unanimity to prevent deadlock without abandoning the consensus spirit).

The key right in CDM is not merely the right to be heard, but the right to block — to prevent a proposal from passing when a participant finds it seriously harmful. When a proposal is blocked, the proposers are invited to modify it to address the concern and achieve genuine consensus. This inverts the logic of majoritarian democracy: rather than allowing a 51% majority to impose its will on 49%, CDM creates structural pressure toward proposals that can be supported across lines of difference.

This distinction matters enormously. Majoritarian democracy optimizes for winning. CDM optimizes for proposals that are genuinely equitable. The difference is not procedural but political — it changes what kinds of outcomes are possible and who is able to shape them.

## Anarchist Cybernetics (Swann 2021)

Tom Swann's *Anarchist Cybernetics* (2021) analyzes Occupy movement decision-making through the lens of cybernetic systems theory, and derives from that analysis a set of recommendations for radical digital platforms.

Swann identifies three core functions that such platforms should serve:

1. **Aid for offline organizing** — Digital tools should support and extend face-to-face organizing rather than replace it. The platform is infrastructure for communities, not a substitute for them.
2. **Externalizing cognitive facilities** — The platform should handle memory, coordination, and record-keeping that would otherwise burden participants, freeing cognitive resources for deliberation.
3. **Mechanisms reinforcing collective self-organization** — The platform should make horizontal self-governance easier, not harder — actively supporting the group's capacity to manage itself without external authority.

Swann also identifies design qualities appropriate to radical platforms: reversibility (decisions and configurations can be undone), flexibility (the platform adapts to the group rather than forcing the group to adapt to the platform), balance between ease and depth (accessible enough for broad participation, deep enough for complex governance), and play (the platform should not be grimly instrumental).

Swann draws on Clarke's (1984) normative development principles for appropriate technology, adapted here for digital platform design:

- **Comprehensibility** — Users can understand what the platform does and why
- **Compatibility with aesthetic values** — The platform is not alienating or ugly
- **Feasibility of continual reassessment and fundamental redesign** — Nothing is locked in permanently
- **Multifunctionality** — The platform serves multiple purposes for diverse groups
- **Capacity to fulfill basic human needs** — The platform serves real needs, not manufactured ones
- **Tendency to reduce artificial scarcities** — The platform does not create dependency or lock-in
- **Incompatibility with technocratic/bureaucratic structures** — The platform cannot easily be captured by hierarchy
- **Compatibility with democratic control and decentralized decision-making** — Power flows horizontally
- **Conduciveness to enjoyment, creativity, and human development** — The platform is a site of flourishing, not just function

These principles are directly traceable in ConsensusUX design decisions — particularly the absence of admin privileges, the reversibility of all group settings, and the emphasis on progressive disclosure over complexity.

## Decolonial Design (Smith et al. 2024)

Smith, Lenskjold, Brynjarsdóttir, and colleagues (2024) offer a three-part framework for understanding and practicing decolonial design that directly informs ConsensusUX.

**Decolonising IN design** refers to the work of recognizing and making explicit the frames of reference — epistemological, cultural, political — that any design practice brings. No design is neutral. Every interface encodes assumptions about who the user is, what they want, what they can be trusted with, and how decisions should be made. Making those assumptions visible is the first move.

**Decolonising BY design** foregrounds collaboration as a catalyst. Rather than designing for communities, this approach designs with them, foregrounding alternative epistemologies and ways of knowing that dominant design culture routinely suppresses. The goal is not to include marginalized perspectives as input into an otherwise unchanged process, but to allow those perspectives to reshape the process itself.

**Decolonising THROUGH design** is the most ambitious frame: mobilizing cracks in existing systems, moving beyond research and documentation to support autonomous action. Design becomes a tool for communities to extend their own power and self-determination, not merely to express their preferences within systems they do not control.

This framework connects to anarchist thought through the principle of **prefiguration**: the idea that equitable futures can only be reached through equitable methods. You cannot build horizontal infrastructure through vertical processes. The means must embody the ends. ConsensusUX attempts to instantiate this: the platform was designed through a process that refused the shortcuts (admin privileges, surveillance infrastructure, behavioral nudges) that would have made design easier but produced an authoritarian tool.

## Consensus vs. Adversarial Design

Carl DiSalvo's "adversarial design" framework uses "consensus" as a pejorative — equating it with liberal-democratic stability, hegemonic smoothing-over of conflict, the elimination of the political. DiSalvo draws on Carl Schmitt's friend/enemy distinction (itself a product of Nazi political philosophy) to argue that design should create rupture rather than consensus.

This framing is wrong, and the error is consequential.

Consensus, properly understood, is not liberal-democratic stability. It is not the suppression of conflict into false agreement. It is a set of technologies and practices for mutual empowerment — for communities to administer shared resources and make collective decisions without recourse to coercion or authority. The Occupy general assembly was not an exercise in hegemony. It was an exercise in radical democracy.

The adversarial design framework mistakes its enemy. The problem is not consensus but the liberal-democratic capture of consensus language — the way that markets, states, and platforms claim to represent collective agreement while suppressing the conditions that would make genuine collective agreement possible.

ConsensusUX is not rupture-creating in DiSalvo's sense. It is structure-building: creating the infrastructure for collective self-organization that adversarial design, by refusing to build anything, cannot provide. Audre Lorde's observation that the master's tools will never dismantle the master's house does not mean that no tools should be built — it means that the tools we build must be fundamentally different in kind.
